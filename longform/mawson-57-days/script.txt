=== 00 | INTRO
In 1912, three men walked into Antarctica. Only one walked out. And when he finally saw home... his ship had just sailed away without him.
This is a true story. His name was Douglas Mawson. He was 30 years old, a geologist from Australia, and the leader of an expedition to the coldest, windiest place on Earth.
Their base was a wooden hut at Cape Denison, where the wind can blow at over 200 kilometres an hour. Mawson's men called it the home of the blizzard.
On the 10th of November 1912, he set off east with two friends. Belgrave Ninnis, a young British army officer. And Xavier Mertz, a Swiss mountaineer and ski champion who was always joking.
They had two sledges, 17 dogs, and a plan to map hundreds of kilometres of ice no human had ever seen.
For five weeks, it worked. They crossed two giant glaciers. They climbed over hidden cracks. They were more than 500 kilometres from the hut.
And then came the day everything changed.

=== 01 | DAY 1
Day 1. The 14th of December. Just after 1 in the afternoon.
Mertz is out in front on skis, singing. Mawson is riding the first sledge. Behind him, Ninnis walks beside the second sledge with the strongest dogs.
That second sledge carries the important stuff. The good tent. Most of the men's food. ALL of the dogs' food.
Mertz raises his ski pole. A warning. There's a crack under the snow.
Mawson crosses it. He calls back to Ninnis. And then he turns around.
Behind him there is nothing. No sledge. No dogs. No Ninnis. Just a dark hole in the snow, about 3 metres wide.
They crawl to the edge and look down. Far below, on a ledge, they can see one injured dog and a few bags. The ledge is around 45 metres down. Their ropes don't reach.
They shout for hours. Nobody answers.
Mawson reads the burial service at the edge of the crevasse.
Then they check what they have left. A stove. Some fuel. Their sleeping bags. Six thin, hungry dogs. And food for about 10 days.
They are 500 kilometres from home.
Food for 10 days. A walk home of many weeks. You can already see the problem.

=== 02 | DAY 2
Day 2. They have no tent, so Mertz builds one. He takes a spare tent cover they'd left behind at an old camp, props it up with a ski and two sledge runners, and the two men squeeze inside. It's barely big enough for two.
Now comes the hardest choice of the trip.
They could take the long way home, along the coast, where seals and penguins live. But it's slow, and it's full of glaciers.
Or they could go inland. Higher, colder, empty. Nothing to hunt. But shorter.
Mawson chooses inland. And that means one terrible thing.
The dogs that pulled their sledges... will now have to feed them.
It's the oldest rule of polar travel, and every explorer of that age knew it. They hate it. But without it, they won't make it back.
The weakest dog, George, is the first. That night Mawson writes in his diary that the meat was tough... but that the liver, at least, was tender.
Remember that liver. It's going to matter later.

=== 03 | DAY 8
Day 8. Every day now looks the same. Wake up. Melt snow. Pack the sledge. Walk into the wind until your legs give out.
The dogs are so weak they have to be carried on the sledge at times. One by one, they're gone. Johnson. Mary. Haldane.
The men are now pulling the sledge themselves, leaning into harnesses like horses.
And the cold isn't even the worst part. It's the light.
In Antarctic summer, the sun never sets. The snow is so bright it burns their eyes. So they start marching through the night, when the sun is low and the snow is firmer.
They are starving slowly, and they know it. Each man's ration is tiny, and they're burning thousands of calories just to move.
Mertz tries to stay cheerful. He tells jokes. He talks about Switzerland. About warm cheese and green mountains.
Mawson keeps them on a compass bearing west. Toward the hut. Toward the ship that's coming to take them home.
Because that's the deadline. The ship. The Aurora will arrive in January... and it can't wait forever.

=== 04 | DAY 16
Day 16. The 29th of December. The last dog, Ginger, is gone.
Now it's just two men, one sledge, and the white.
And something is starting to go wrong with their bodies.
Their skin is peeling off in strips. Their hair is falling out in clumps. They feel sick and dizzy, and they can't understand why. They're eating. Not much, but they're eating.
Here's what they didn't know.
Husky liver is packed with vitamin A. So much of it that, in large amounts, it acts like a poison. Peeling skin. Hair loss. Stomach pain. Confusion.
Many scientists today believe that the one food the two men enjoyed... may have been slowly making them ill. Others think it was simply starvation, cold and exhaustion. Nobody can know for sure.
But either way, the two men are getting weaker by the day.
On New Year's Day 1913, Mertz writes the last words in his diary. He says he's really tired... and that he shall write no more.

=== 05 | DAY 19
Day 19. Mertz is in real trouble now.
His fingers are frostbitten. He's barely eating. He hasn't the strength to walk for long.
Mawson decides they'll rest. Maybe a day of sleep will help.
It doesn't.
Here's the cruel part. Mertz was a champion skier. Out on the ice, he had always led the way. And now he can barely stand.
For days they move in tiny steps. A few kilometres. Then less. Then barely anything.
Mawson has a decision to make, and he knows it. On his own, he might still get back. With Mertz, maybe neither of them will.
He stays.
On the 7th of January, Mertz can no longer walk at all. Mawson lifts him onto the sledge, wraps him in his sleeping bag, and pulls him himself.
They don't get far. Mawson puts up the tiny tent. And he sits with his friend through the night.

=== 06 | DAY 26
Day 26. The 8th of January. About 2 in the morning.
Xavier Mertz dies quietly in his sleeping bag. In his diary, Mawson writes that he died peacefully.
Mawson is now completely alone. More than 160 kilometres from the hut. No dogs. No friend. And food for only a few days more.
He buries Mertz in the snow, makes a cross from two pieces of the sledge, and reads the burial service again.
And then he does something that tells you exactly who Douglas Mawson was.
He takes his knife... and cuts the sledge in half.
Half a sledge is half the weight. He makes a sail from Mertz's coat and a bag, so the wind can push him along. He boils the last of the meat so he can save fuel.
He's not waiting to be rescued. Nobody even knows where he is. If he's going to get home, he has to walk there.

=== 07 | DAY 29
Day 29. Mawson starts walking. He gets about 3 kilometres before he notices his feet feel strange. Squishy. Wrong.
He stops, sits down, and pulls off his socks.
The soles of both his feet have come away... in one complete layer.
Months of cold, sweat and hunger have done this. Under them, his feet are raw.
Any normal person stops here. Mawson rubs the old soles with lanolin, a kind of grease from sheep's wool, puts them back where they belong, pulls socks over them, and laces up his boots again.
And he keeps walking.
About 8 kilometres a day now. Some days less. That night he writes that his whole body seems to be rotting from lack of food.
He's 30 years old. He looks decades older.
But in front of him is the biggest obstacle of the whole journey. A giant glacier, cracked like broken glass, hiding crevasses under every step. The same kind of crack that swallowed Ninnis.

=== 08 | DAY 35
Day 35. The 17th of January. Mawson is crossing the glacier, dragging his half-sledge.
Then the snow under his feet disappears.
He falls into a crevasse. Straight down into the dark.
And then, with a jerk, he stops. The rope around his waist has caught. The sledge, at the top, has jammed against the edge.
He is hanging in the air, inside a crack in the ice, spinning slowly on a rope. Below him is a drop he cannot see the bottom of.
He later wrote that his first thought was, so this is the end.
His second thought was, not yet.
He grabs the rope. He pulls himself up, hand over hand. His fingers are numb. He reaches the edge... and the snow breaks. He falls again.
Exhausted, he thinks about simply letting go. It would be quick. It would be over.
Instead, he climbs again. And this time, he drags himself out.
That night, he makes a rope ladder and ties it to the sledge, so if it ever happens again, he can climb out more easily. And it does happen again. Twice more. And twice more, he climbs out.

=== 09 | DAY 47
Day 47. The 29th of January. Mawson is on his last scraps of food.
And then he sees something that shouldn't be there.
A dark shape on the snow, about 300 metres to his right. Not a rock. Something made by people.
It's a snow cairn, a little tower of snow, with a black cloth tied on top so it can be seen from far away. A search party left it.
Inside is food. And a note.
The note says his friends are searching for him. And it tells him that a place called Aladdin's Cave is only around 35 kilometres away.
But he reads one more thing. The men who built this cairn... left it that very same morning.
He missed them by just a few hours.
If he'd been a few hours faster, he'd have walked home with friends.
But he has food now. And somewhere ahead, the ship should be waiting.
The question is, will it still be there?

=== 10 | DAY 50
Day 50. The 1st of February. After three hard days crossing slippery blue ice, Mawson reaches Aladdin's Cave. It's a little ice cave his team dug into the slope, about 9 kilometres above the hut.
Inside, he finds three oranges and a pineapple.
Fresh fruit, in Antarctica. It can only mean one thing. The ship really is here.
He is so close. Just a few hours' walk from home.
And then the blizzard comes.
The famous wind of Cape Denison slams the mountain. Walking outside would mean being blown off the slope and lost forever.
So Mawson waits. One day. Two days. Five days. A whole week, alone in an ice cave, listening to the wind... knowing his ship might be leaving right now.
He uses the time well. With nails and wood from packing boxes, he makes his own spikes for his boots, so he won't slip on the ice.
And on the 8th of February, the wind finally drops.

=== 11 | DAY 57
Day 57. Mawson walks out of the cave and down the slope toward the hut.
Below him he can see the bay. The water. The hut.
He looks out at the bay for the ship.
The bay is empty.
The Aurora had waited for weeks. It had searched the coast for him. And it sailed away just a few hours before he arrived.
Then he sees three men working near the hut. They look up. They see a tiny figure coming down the hill... and they run up to meet him.
They are men from his own team who volunteered to stay behind for another winter and keep looking for him.
One of them reportedly asked him, which one are you?
They signal the ship by radio. It turns back. But the wind is so fierce no boat can reach the shore, and the ship cannot stay. It sails away again. Mawson will spend another 10 months in Antarctica.
Later he said he probably could not have survived the sea voyage anyway. His body needed those months to heal.
He went home in 1914, married his fiancée Paquita, and was knighted.
Years later, Sir Edmund Hillary, one of the first two men to climb Everest, called it probably the greatest story of lone survival in polar exploration.
57 days. 2 friends lost. Half a sledge. And a man who refused to stop walking.
Which day would you have given up?
